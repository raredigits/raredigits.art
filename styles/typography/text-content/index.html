Heading

Body Text

Body text is the default. Paragraphs sit on the text-content column at a comfortable measure, the line-height is tuned for sustained reading, and nothing extra is needed — just write.

.lead

A single oversized opening paragraph. Think of it as the page's TL;DR: one or two sentences that tell the reader what this page is about and whether to keep reading. Use it once, at the top of a long-read.

Typography is not decoration; it is the path the reader walks. A well-set page tells the reader, before a single word is parsed, that the content was thought about. Selector .lead is how you say that out loud.

The treatment is intentionally restrained — larger size, lighter weight, slightly tighter leading. Loud enough to set the page, quiet enough to step out of the way.

Special Text Elements

A handful of blocks sit slightly past the text-content column on both sides — a small but deliberate signal that they are not the prose, they are about the prose. Captions, highlights, and content-captions all share this outdent: var(--space-lg) on desktop, tightening to var(--space-sm) on mobile. The shift is driven by a single internal SCSS mixin (@mixin outdent in typography/_text-content.scss) so the three blocks stay coherent. In v0.6.15 the same pattern will be exposed as the public utility .rd-outdent.

.text-content-caption

A wrapper that widens its content past the regular text column by one outdent on each side. Use it for figures, illustrative cards, or any block that needs slightly more horizontal room than a paragraph but should still belong to the article.

.highlight

An emphatic, centered block with a thick brand-color border. The reading flow stops here and reads this carefully — so use it sparingly, for the single sentence on the page that has to land.

Make it beautiful by making it understandable. Not loud. Not clever. Just right.

.caption

An aside-style block with a soft gray background and a brand-color left rule. Used for editor's notes, observations, or short asides that comment on the prose without being part of it.

A bad font doesn't just look bad. It feels irrelevant.

<mark>

Inline highlighting for words or phrases inside running text. Use it to flag the single phrase a skimming reader must not miss.

<cite>

The semantic element for attributing a quotation. Renders right-aligned and muted — see the <blockquote> example below.

Blockquote

A good quote is like a well-timed assist in basketball—it brings someone else’s brilliance into play exactly when you need it. Blockquotes give voice and weight to borrowed wisdom, letting other people’s words do the heavy lifting while you focus on connecting the dots.

The quote is a strange literary device. It allows us to borrow authority we haven’t earned, to dress our modest thoughts in borrowed finery.

Yet there’s honesty in the quote, too. It admits that all our ideas are built on foundations laid by others. Every writer is, in some sense, a plagiarist—not by theft, but by inheritance.

Jorge Luis Borges, “The Library of Babel and Other Essays”

In Rare Styles, <blockquote> are set apart with comfortable margins and a subtle background treatment—enough to signal “this is someone else talking” without turning your page into a ransom note made of clippings. Citations are handled through the <cite> element, because proper attribution is the difference between scholarship and plagiarism, and we prefer the former.

Epigraph

An <figure class="epigraph"> sets a short, attributed quotation at the head of a section or page. Unlike a blockquote, it is small, italic, and right-aligned — a voice from outside the text, placed at the threshold rather than woven into the argument.

Keep epigraphs brief. Their job is to set a tone before the reader enters, not to carry an argument.

.boilerplate

A muted block for text notes, fine print, or a short standing notice, set off from the prose by a top rule and a leading info marker. Each <p> inside carries the marker, so the block reads as a small notice about the page rather than part of the argument. The glyph is drawn from the library’s SVG icon set (an SVG mask on ::before). Harvested into the library in v0.6.15.

Rare Styles is a narrow, professional CSS library for clarity-first longreads and decision-first data views — not a general-purpose framework.

Definition Lists

A plain <dl> is styled directly: readable spacing, bold enlarged terms, and extra separation before each new term. Use it for glossary-style or principle-style content where each term introduces a short block — the manifesto principles are set exactly this way.

Inline Elements

The small semantic elements of running prose. Each is styled on its own tag, so the right element is all the markup there is.

<kbd>

A key to press, drawn as a keycap so it is not mistaken for code, which is something to type. Press Ctrl + K to search. A whole combination can be one element with the keys nested inside: Shift+Tab.

<abbr>

An abbreviation with its expansion in title: the CPI rose for the third month. The dotted line hints that there is more on hover — but touch screens have no hover, so spell the term out in the text the first time it appears and use the bare abbreviation after that.

<sub> and <sup>

Set on the baseline without opening up the line: CO2 emissions, 106 tonnes, the 1st quarter.

References and notes

A reference mark is a <sup> holding a link to its note; the note links back. Plain ids and links do the work — no script, and the browser's own back button returns the reader to the text.1 The note the reader jumped to is marked while it is the target.2

  1. Each note's id matches the mark's link, and its back link points at the mark's id. ↩
  2. The fixed header is accounted for: the page scrolls the target clear of it. ↩
<p>… the text.<sup><a href="#note-1" id="ref-1" aria-label="Note 1">1</a></sup></p>

<div class="footnote">
    <ol>
        <li id="note-1">The note. <a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Back to reference 1">↩</a></li>
    </ol>
</div>

Numbered automatically, with one source of truth for the order, is a full footnote engine — that comes later. For an article with a handful of notes, numbering by hand is fine.

Figures

A <figure> inside an <article> is styled directly: it sits on the reading measure, its image fills that measure, and its <figcaption> reads as a quiet note under it. Outside an article, add .figure for the same treatment — in a card, a section or an island. Carousel slides and epigraphs are figures too, but keep their own layout.

.figure-wide

A wide figure steps out to the caption width the lead and the highlight use. Its caption stays on the reading measure. Inside a card or a collapsible it simply fills the content width.

.figure-source

The source or credit line, set on its own line in a smaller size at the end of the caption. A long URL in it breaks rather than widening the page.

<figure class="figure-wide">
    <img src="chart.png" alt="What the image shows" width="1648" height="720" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>
        The caption says what to take from it.
        <span class="figure-source">Source: …</span>
    </figcaption>
</figure>

Images that load well

  • Always set width and height to the image's real pixel size. The browser reserves the box from them, so the text does not jump when the image arrives; CSS still scales the image to the column.
  • Write alt for what the image says in this article, not what it looks like in general. A purely decorative image gets alt="". The caption is not a substitute: it is read after the image, and only when there is one.
  • Add loading="lazy" below the fold, never on the first image a reader sees.
  • Offer sizes with srcset and sizes. The ordinary figure is at most the reading measure (about 640 px on desktop, the full screen on a phone): sizes="(width < 769px) 100vw, 640px". Producing the image files is your build's job — the library only lays them out.
